# CAM for forgeCAD: a survey of the literature

This is research only. Nothing in the kernel uses it yet. It asks what the CAM literature already
offers that could be specified in Lean over the reals and computed exactly or certified, by the
levels in research/PROOF-STANDARD.md (L1 spec, L2 exact reference, L3 correct on a stated domain,
L4 refinement, L5 proven float predicates, L6 differential fuzz). Every source was looked up on
2026-11-23.

Source marks:
- **[R]**: I read the abstract, the author's page or the paper this session.
- **[S]**: I saw only a search-engine summary or a bibliographic record this session.
- **[K]**: known to me but not looked up again. Check the citation before relying on it.

In the summaries, CL means cutter location (the tool reference point the controller is given), CC
means cutter contact (the point where the tool touches the surface), a_e is the radial depth (step
over), a_p is the axial depth, D is the tool diameter and f_z is the feed per tooth.

---

## 1. 2.5D toolpaths: offsets, zigzag, linking

**The two families.** Direction-parallel (zigzag or zig) paths are parallel lines clipped to the
pocket. They are simple, but every reversal has a sharp corner, the tool alternates between climb
and conventional cutting, and material is left against the walls unless a finishing contour follows.
Contour-parallel paths are successive inward offsets of the boundary. They keep the cutting
direction the same and follow the walls, but they need a robust offset, linking between the loops,
and handling where the offsets split.

- **Held, M. (1991). *On the Computational Geometry of Pocket Machining*.** LNCS 500, Springer.
  doi:10.1007/3-540-54103-9. [S] (Springer, TAMU catalogue)
  This is the standard monograph. It treats pocketing as computational geometry: the Voronoi
  diagram (medial axis) of the pocket and its islands is the structure for both interrogating the
  shape and planning the path. Offsets at any distance can be read off the Voronoi diagram. The
  topology of the offset loops forms a tree, which gives the order in which the loops are cut and
  the places where they must be linked.
- **Held, Lukács, Andor (1994). "Pocket machining based on contour-parallel tool paths generated by
  means of proximity maps."** *CAD* 26(3):189–203;
  cited on Held's project page [R] https://www.cosy.sbg.ac.at/~held/projects/pocket_off/pocket.html.
  It generates offsets from a proximity map (the Voronoi diagram), uses variable step-over, and
  claims gouge-free machining of the whole pocket. The page makes no claim about numerical
  robustness.
- **Held, M. (1998). "Voronoi diagrams and offset curves of curvilinear polygons."** *CAD*
  30(4):287–300. doi:10.1016/S0010-4485(97)00071-7. [S]
  It extends the method to boundaries made of segments and circular arcs. The offset of such a
  boundary is again made of segments and arcs, so the representation is closed under offsetting.
- **Held, M. (2001). "VRONI: an engineering approach to the reliable and efficient computation of
  Voronoi diagrams of points and line segments."** *Computational Geometry* 18(2):95–123. [K]
  Topology-oriented incremental insertion with a run of fall-backs and relaxed epsilons, in floating
  point. It is reliable in practice but not proven. This is the counter-example to a
  "decide once" rule: it is a chain of retries.
- **Held & Huber (2009). "Topology-oriented incremental computation of Voronoi diagrams of circular
  arcs and straight-line segments."** *CAD* 41(5):327–338. doi:10.1016/j.cad.2008.08.004 [K for
  DOI]. [S] (dblp, author PDF https://www.sthu.org/research/publications/files/HeHu09.pdf)
  This is ArcVRONI. It extends Sugihara–Iri topology-oriented insertion to genuine circular arcs,
  without polygonising them.
- **Kim, D.-S. (1998). "Polygon offsetting using a Voronoi diagram and two stacks."** *CAD*
  30(14):1069–1076. doi:10.1016/S0010-4485(98)00063-3. [S]
  Given the Voronoi diagram, it walks the offset with two synchronised stacks to produce the trimmed
  offset (the part of the raw offset that keeps its distance). It is simple and linear once the
  diagram exists.
- **Aichholzer, Aurenhammer, Alberts, Gärtner (1995). "A novel type of skeleton for polygons."**
  *J. Universal Computer Science* 1(12):752–761. doi:10.3217/jucs-001-12-0752. [S]
  This is the straight skeleton. It is the trace of the vertices while the edges move inward at unit
  speed, and it is made only of straight bisector pieces. Its offsets are mitred: a reflex corner
  stays sharp instead of rounding. That is the wrong shape for a round cutter's centre path, where
  the true offset rounds the corner. It does give exact rational geometry for line-only input if
  the edge directions are rational and unit-speed is replaced by a weighted speed (a weighted
  skeleton).
- **CGAL, "2D Straight Skeleton and Polygon Offsetting"** (Cacciola, later Loriot and
  Rouxel-Labbé). https://doc.cgal.org/6.1/Straight_skeleton_2/index.html [S]
  It builds the interior skeleton of a polygon with holes, and the offsets come from the skeleton.
  It is mitred, as above. It has weighted skeletons since 5.6, and it runs with exact kernels.
- **CGAL 2D Minkowski sums / offset polygons (Wein).** https://doc.cgal.org/5.4/Minkowski_sum_2/index.html
  and R. Wein, PhD thesis, Tel Aviv 2007
  https://www.cgl.cs.tau.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/WeinPhdThesis.pdf. [S]
  It computes the offset of a polygon (its Minkowski sum with a disc) as an exact curve arrangement
  of the convolution cycles and keeps the cells with nonzero winding number. CGAL can give the
  offset exactly, with arcs whose coordinates are in a quadratic extension, or approximate it with a
  guaranteed error bound. This is the nearest existing exact, rounded offset to what forgeCAD needs.
- **Park & Choi (2001). "Uncut free pocketing tool-paths generation using pair-wise offset
  algorithm."** *CAD* 33(10):739–746. [S]
  It offsets pairs of elements and resolves self-intersections (the PWID algorithm), then adds
  clean-up passes only where uncut regions exist. See §3.

**Linking, lead-in and lead-out.** These are mostly practice: tangent arc leads, ramp or helix
entries, and keeping the tool down between loops. Held (1991) and Park & Chung, "Offset
tool-path linking for pocket machining" (*CAD* 34(4), 2002 [S]), are the geometric references.
A lead arc is a segment-and-arc construction, so it stays inside the line-and-arc algebra.

**For an exact implementation.** The trimmed offset of a segment-and-arc boundary at distance d is
exact. A segment offsets to a segment, an arc to a concentric arc of radius r ± d, and a convex
vertex to an arc of radius d about the vertex. The new endpoints are intersections of lines and
circles, so their coordinates are in quadratic extensions. Each further offset can add another
square root, so the degree grows with the number of passes unless the directions are kept
Pythagorean (rational unit normals). The trim, which drops pieces nearer than d to the boundary, is
the same regularised-boolean decision forgeCAD already proves (union with a disc). A Voronoi-based
method needs exact predicates for segments and arcs. The arc predicates are the hard ones: CGAL's
segment Delaunay graph does segments exactly (Karavelas 2004 [K]), but I know of no exact,
certified arc Voronoi in production.

## 2. Adaptive, trochoidal and constant-engagement clearing

The aim is to hold the radial engagement angle (the angle the tool spends in the material each
revolution) near a set value, so the tool load is steady, deep axial cuts are safe, and there is no
overload at inside corners. On a straight cut, the engagement is φ = arccos(1 − 2a_e/D). At an
inside corner of an offset path, φ rises sharply, up to 180° in a slot.

- **Stori & Wright (2000). "Constant engagement tool path generation for convex geometries."**
  *J. Manufacturing Systems* 19(3):172–184. [S]
  A spiral-in path that tracks engagement, path curvature, and entry and exit angles, and produces a
  C² path. Where constant engagement cannot be held, it controls curvature instead. It works only
  for convex pockets, and later authors note it can leave material in corners.
- **Choy & Chan (2002), corner-looping paths,** *IJAMT* (s001700200232); and **(2003) "Modeling
  cutter swept angle at cornering cut,"** *Int. J. CAD/CAM* 3. https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200336037347979.page [S]
  An analytic model of the "cutter swept angle" (the engagement angle) through a corner, and loop
  paths that add a small loop at a corner to cap the engagement.
- **Bieterman & Sandstrom (2003). "A curvilinear tool-path method for pocket machining."** *J.
  Manuf. Sci. Eng.* 125(4):709–715. doi:10.1115/1.1596579 [K for DOI]. [S]
  The path is the level sets of the solution of a Laplace (elliptic boundary-value) problem on the
  pocket, so it changes smoothly from a near-circle at the centre to the boundary shape. It is
  reported to give about 30% shorter machining time and doubled tool life in titanium (Boeing). It
  suits convex pockets and has no islands. The PDE solution is numerical, so this method is
  inherently approximate.
- **Elber, Cohen, Drake (2005). "MATHSM: medial axis transform toward high speed machining of
  pockets."** *CAD* 37(2):241–250. doi:10.1016/j.cad.2004.05.008. [S] (Technion preprint
  https://gershon.cs.technion.ac.il/papers/hsm.pdf)
  Offset and zigzag paths are not C¹. This method builds a C¹ path of mostly circular arcs (biarcs),
  taken from the medial axis transform, with radii as large as possible to keep the radial
  acceleration low. It suits long, narrow pockets.
- **Kardes & Altintas, circular milling.** N. Kardes, *Mechanics and dynamics of circular milling
  operations*, UBC thesis 2005, https://circle.ubc.ca/handle/2429/16538 [S]. The journal version
  (Kardes & Altintas 2007, *J. Manuf. Sci. Eng.* 129) is [K]: I could not confirm the DOI.
  It gives the chip thickness and forces when the tool centre follows a circle (as in trochoidal and
  circular interpolation), and extends Altintas–Budak stability to that case.
- **Ibaraki, Yamaji, Matsubara (2010). "On the removal of critical cutting regions by trochoidal
  grooving."** *Precision Engineering* 34(3):467–473. [S]
  https://mecdes.hiroshima-u.ac.jp/~ibaraki.h0523/pubs/abstract/pe10_yamaji.html
  It finds the regions where an offset path would overload the tool (corners and narrow necks) from
  the medial axis, and cuts only those with a trochoid, also built on the medial axis. The 2003 ICAM
  papers (1st and 2nd reports) give the Voronoi-based trochoid.
- **Abrahamsen, M. (2014/2015). "Spiral toolpaths for high-speed machining of 2D pockets with or
  without islands."** arXiv:1412.5034 (v4 20 Jan 2015); ASME IDETC 2015; *J. Comp. Des. Eng.*
  2019. [R] https://arxiv.org/abs/1412.5034
  The spiral is made of segments and arcs, is G¹, does not cross itself, and its successive turns
  are at most δ apart. These are three properties stated as guarantees, which makes it the most
  "spec-shaped" paper in this area. Islands are handled by growing the spiral out from the island.
- **Commercial "adaptive" clearing** (Autodesk Adaptive, Mastercam Dynamic, FreeCAD Adaptive). [K]
  These are mostly unpublished. They march a tool circle against a rasterised or Clipper-polygon
  model of the remaining stock, choosing each step so that the engaged arc stays under a limit.

**Computing the radial engagement.** Engagement is the angle of the tool circle that lies inside
the remaining stock. Spence & Altintas (1994, §7) did it on CSG. The Mitsubishi Electric Research Laboratories ADF work (Erdim &
Sullivan 2012, TR2012-093 [S]) does it on distance fields. Most CAM does it on rasters.
**Chip thinning.** [K] With a_e < D/2 the largest chip is h_max = f_z·sin φ_e =
2 f_z·√((a_e/D)(1 − a_e/D)), so the feed is raised by D/(2√(a_e(D − a_e))) to get back the nominal
chip load. A lead angle κ adds a further factor of sin κ.

**For an exact implementation.** Engagement is a geometric quantity: the arcs of the tool circle
cut by the stock boundary. If the stock is a line-and-arc region, those intersections are
quadratic-extension numbers and φ is an exact angle (keep it as a cosine or chord, not radians).
So "engagement ≤ φ_max along the path" is exactly decidable at sample points. Between samples it
needs a Lipschitz or interval argument. PDE spirals (Bieterman) and marching adaptive clearing can
only be checked afterwards, not proven correct by construction.

## 3. Rest machining and uncut regions

An uncut region is left when the step-over exceeds what one pass reaches. For offset paths at
interval s with tool radius r, it happens when r < s < 2r at corners (Park & Choi 2001 [S]). Rest
machining is the general case: find material that a previous larger tool could not reach, and clear
it with a smaller one.

- **Held (1991)**, Voronoi-based. The region a tool of radius r reaches is the union of discs of
  radius r centred on the offset of the boundary at distance r, which is the morphological opening.
  The uncut region is pocket ∖ opening_r(pocket). [S]
- **Park & Choi (2001)** [S]: clean-up only where uncut regions exist.
- **Pencil and rest-finishing in 3D:** see §4. Concave creases where the tool touches two surfaces
  at once (double contact) are traced as pencil curves.

**For an exact implementation.** In 2D the reached region is a morphological opening: an erosion
by a disc followed by a dilation by a disc. Both are offsets, so the rest region is exact, built
from lines and arcs. What a tool *actually* removed follows from the swept volume (§6) minus stock,
which is the same boolean. "No uncut region larger than ε" is then a decidable property of an exact
region (its inradius, or its distance to the part). This is a good first L1 target: the spec is
removed = ⋃_t (tool at t) ∩ stock, and rest = stock ∖ removed ∖ part.

## 4. 3-axis surface finishing

- **Choi & Jerard (1998). *Sculptured Surface Machining: Theory and Applications*.** Kluwer.
  doi:10.1007/978-1-4615-5283-3. [S]
  The standard text: the CL surface (C-space), the inverse tool offset, Z-maps, gouge, and scallop.
- **CL surface / inverse tool offset / C-space.** [S] (Choi, Kim, Jerard 1997, cited in Korean ITO
  literature https://koreascience.kr/article/JAKO200711850422644.pdf)
  For a 3-axis tool with a fixed axis, the set of safe CL points is the Minkowski sum of the part
  with the inverted tool (the tool reflected through its CL point). Its upper surface is the CL
  surface. Drop-cutter samples it: at each (x, y), it lowers the tool until it first touches,
  z_CL(x, y) = max over part features of the contact height. The inverse offset method (ITO)
  computes it by placing the inverted tool on the part points.
- **Drop-cutter against triangles: OpenCAMLib** (A. Wallin). https://github.com/aewallin/opencamlib,
  https://opencamlib.readthedocs.io/ [S]
  Drop-cutter tests each triangle's vertices, facet and edges and keeps the maximum. Push-cutter
  moves the tool along a "fiber" at constant z, and waterline is built from those fibers. The cutters
  are cylindrical, ball, bull (toroidal), cone and composite. Contact with a ball or cylinder has a
  closed form. Bull-nose against an edge needs a quartic and is solved numerically there.
- **Yau, Chuang, Lee (2004). "Numerical control machining of triangulated sculptured surfaces in a
  stereo lithography format with a generalized cutter."** *IJPR* 42(13). [S] (record only; the
  co-authors are [K])
  Drop-cutter for the generalised APT cutter: seven parameters that cover flat, ball, bull, cone and
  taper tools. The APT cutter is bounded by planes, cones, cylinders and a torus, so it is a CSG of
  surfaces forgeCAD already has. Chuang and Yau have related reverse-engineering and tri-dexel
  papers [K].
- **Waterline / push-cutter / Z-level.** [S] (OpenCAMLib; FreeCAD CAM_Waterline wiki)
  Waterline is the slice z = const of the CL surface: the offset at that height of the part's
  cross-section by the tool's horizontal radius at that depth. It is a 2D offset problem at each z.
- **Suresh & Yang (1994). "Constant scallop-height machining of free-form surfaces."** *J. Eng.
  Ind.* 116(2):253–259. doi:10.1115/1.2901938. [S]
  Choose each next pass so that the scallop height (the ridge between passes) is constant rather
  than the spacing. It gives shorter CL data and less machining time.
- **Lin & Koren (1996). "Efficient tool-path planning for machining free-form surfaces."** *J. Eng.
  Ind.* 118:20–28. [S] (citation only)
  Iso-scallop paths from a chosen first path, each next path offset by the width that gives the
  scallop height h.
- **Scallop height.** [K] For a ball of radius R on a flat surface with step-over s,
  h = R − √(R² − s²/4). On a curved surface the effective radius is changed by the normal curvature
  κ across the feed: R_eff with 1/R_eff = 1/R ± κ, for convex or concave. Both are closed forms in
  R, s and κ.
- **Pencil tracing.** [K] (Choi & Jerard; Ren, Yau, Lee 2004 "Clean-up tool path generation by
  contraction tool method", *IJPR*)
  Pencil curves are the creases of the CL surface, where the tool touches two surfaces at once
  (double contact). Bitangent curves of the part with the tool are solved per pair of surfaces.
- **Anderson, R. O. (1978). "Detecting and eliminating collisions in NC machining."** *CAD*
  10(4):231–237. [K] The original Z-map (a height field on a grid).

**For an exact implementation.** Drop-cutter on planar facets has closed forms for cylinder, ball
and cone tools: point contact is a square root, facet contact is linear for a ball centre at
distance R from the plane, and edge contact is a quadratic. For a plane or quadric part face
against a ball or cylinder tool, the CL surface is an offset quadric, with closed forms (an offset
sphere or cylinder is again a sphere or cylinder; an offset cone is not a cone, but still
algebraic). A torus (bull) against an edge, or any tool against a freeform surface, is a root of
higher degree or a transcendental equation: it needs certified isolation (Krawczyk or interval
Newton, as forgeCAD's intersection tracing already does). Drop-cutter is a max over contacts, so a
certified *upper* bound on z_CL at a sample is enough for gouge safety at that sample. Between
samples, the CL surface is Lipschitz and the CL path can still gouge, so a sampled drop-cutter is
not a proof unless the whole segment's swept volume is checked (§6).

## 5. 5-axis tool orientation

- **Lead and tilt, curvature matching.** [K] A flat or bull tool tilted by a lead angle λ along the
  feed gives an effective cutting profile (an ellipse for a flat end) that can match the surface's
  curvature, the "sturz" method (Vickers & Quan 1989). Larger strips mean fewer passes.
- **Flank (swarf) milling of ruled surfaces:**
  - **Menzel, Bedi, Mann (2004). "Triple tangent flank milling of ruled surfaces."** *CAD*
    36(3):289–296. [S] https://cs.uwaterloo.ca/~smann/Papers/CAD-03.pdf
    The cutter is tangent to the surface at three points, which cuts the error of the two-point
    method.
  - **Senatore, Monies, Landon, Rubio (2008). "Optimising positioning of the axis of a milling
    cutter on an offset surface by geometric error minimisation."** *IJAMT* [S]
    https://hal-univ-tlse3.archives-ouvertes.fr/hal-01913408 (the year and venue are [K]).
    It treats the axis as a line to be optimised against the offset surface to minimise the
    geometric error.
  The common result: only a developable ruled surface can be flank-milled exactly by a cylinder.
  A non-developable one leaves an error that can be bounded but not made zero. That is a proven
  impossibility, so the honest spec states the error bound.
- **Barrel and curvature-adapted cutters.** **Bartoň, Bizzarri, Rist, Sliusarenko, Pottmann (2021).
  "Geometry and tool motion planning for curvature adapted CNC machining."** *ACM TOG* 40(4).
  doi:10.1145/3450626.3459837. [S]
  It uses second-order line contact between the tool and the surface, with a toroidal or barrel
  cutter whose negatively curved region matches the surface, and optimises the positions and the
  motion together. The related tangent-plane ("barrel cutter") methods are in the same line of work
  (Pottmann group, KAUST and TU Wien [S]).
- **Accessibility and visibility:**
  - **Elber, G. (1994). "Accessibility in 5-axis milling environment."** *CAD* 26(11):796–802.
    [S] It reduces 5-axis accessibility for a flat tool to a set of 3-axis accessibility problems.
    Elber & Cohen later unified this for freeform surfaces [S].
  - **Balasubramaniam, Ho, Sarma, Adachi (2002). "Generation of collision-free 5-axis tool paths
    using a haptic surface."** *CAD* 34(4):267–279. [S]
  - **Balasubramaniam, Sarma, Marciniak (2003). "Collision-free finishing toolpaths from
    visibility data."** *CAD* 35(4):359–374. [S]
    Per-point visibility cones on the sphere of directions (from GPU rendering), then a
    collision-free choice of axis respecting machine limits and tilt. This is sampled: it is a
    heuristic, not a certificate.
- **Kinematic smoothness and singularity.** **Affouard, Duc, Lartigue, Langeron, Bourdet (2004).
  "Avoiding 5-axis singularities using tool path deformation."** *IJMTM* 44(4):415–425.
  doi:10.1016/j.ijmachtools.2003.10.008. [S]
  Near the pole (tool axis parallel to the primary rotary axis) a small change in orientation needs
  a large rotary move. The paper deforms the path out of a "singular cone" and reports about 50%
  less time on a high-speed milling example.
- **Post-processing and kinematics: Lee & She (1997). "Developing a postprocessor for three types of
  five-axis machine tools."** *IJAMT* 13:658–665. doi:10.1007/BF01350824. [S]
  Inverse kinematics by homogeneous transforms for table-tilting, spindle-tilting and mixed
  machines. **RTCP / TCPM** (rotation tool centre point, as in Fanuc G43.4 and Siemens TRAORI) moves
  the inverse kinematics into the controller, so the program gives the tool tip and axis in the
  part frame. [K]

**For an exact implementation.** Orientation choice is an optimisation, so do not prove it optimal.
Prove the *result* safe (§6). Inverse kinematics are rotations: exact if the angles are kept as
rational points on the unit circle (via the tan-half-angle), and the controller's interpolation
between two poses (linear in joint angles) is transcendental in Cartesian space. That is the main
reason 5-axis swept volumes need certified intervals and not closed forms.

## 6. Gouge and collision checking; swept volumes

- **Envelope theory / SEDE: Blackmore, Leu, Wang (1997). "The sweep-envelope differential equation
  algorithm and its application to NC machining verification."** *CAD* 29(9):629–637.
  doi:10.1016/S0010-4485(96)00101-7. [S]
  The boundary of a swept solid is traced by integrating an ODE from the grazing points (where the
  velocity is tangent to the tool surface) at the start. It needs grazing points only once, and the
  boundary points come out connected. The ODE is integrated numerically.
- **Jacobian rank deficiency: Abdel-Malek & Yeh (1997). "Geometric representation of the swept
  volume using Jacobian rank-deficiency conditions."** *CAD* 29(6):457–468.
  doi:10.1016/S0010-4485(96)00097-8. [S] Follow-ups are Abdel-Malek, Yeh, Othman 1998 (voids, *CAD*
  30(13)), Abdel-Malek, Yang, Blackmore 2001 (implicit surfaces, *CAD* 33(1)), and Yang &
  Abdel-Malek 2006 (NC verification, *IJAMT* 28).
  The envelope is where the Jacobian of (motion parameter, surface parameters) ↦ R³ drops rank.
  That is a polynomial system when the motion and surfaces are polynomial or rational, so it can be
  solved symbolically in principle.
- **Du, Surmann, Webber, Weinert (2005). "Formulating swept profiles for five-axis tool motions."**
  *IJMTM* 45(7):849–861. [S] (this is the paper the brief's "Weinert" points to: the record found
  differs in authors and year from the query)
  Closed-form swept profiles of APT-type tools under 5-axis motion, from envelope theory, giving
  topologically valid swept volumes.
- **Ilushin, Elber, Halperin, Wein, Kim (2005). "Precise global collision detection in multi-axis
  NC-machining."** *CAD* 37(9):909–920. doi:10.1016/j.cad.2004.09.018. [S]
  Space subdivision along the tool axis (from ray tracing) for any tool shape and holder. Later
  papers note it checks only at discrete positions, not along the motion. A companion paper on
  continuous path verification appeared in *IJCGA* 2005 [S].
- **Lee & Chang (1995). "2-phase approach to global tool interference avoidance in 5-axis
  machining."** *CAD* 27(10):715–729. doi:10.1016/0010-4485(94)00021-5. [S] Convex hulls for a fast
  first pass, then exact local checks. Lee 1997 (*CAD* 29(7)) adds admissible orientations against
  gouging [S].
- **Continuous collision detection (robotics and graphics).** [K] Conservative advancement and
  interval-arithmetic CCD (Redon et al. 2002; Zhang, Lee, Kim "C2A" 2009; Tang/Manocha). These give
  certified "no contact over [t0, t1]" with a bounded refinement, using bounds on the motion's
  speed. This is the certified tool forgeCAD would use for the 5-axis case.

**Exact versus approximate.** In 3 axes the tool's orientation is fixed, so the swept volume along
a line is a Minkowski sum T ⊕ segment. A ball gives a capsule (cylinder plus two spheres), a flat
cylinder gives a slab bounded by planes and cylinders, and a bull gives a torus-capped version. In a
horizontal arc (G2/G3 in the XY plane), the sweep is bounded by tori and spheres or cylinders,
which forgeCAD's curved boolean already targets. A helical move (G2 with Z) or any 5-axis move with
rotation gives a non-algebraic envelope, and only certified enclosures exist. Holder, collet and
spindle are the same problem with larger solids, and they must be checked against the *current*
stock (§7) and the fixtures, not only the part.

## 7. Stock simulation

- **Van Hook, T. (1986). "Real-time shaded NC milling display."** *SIGGRAPH '86* / *Computer
  Graphics* 20(4):15–20. doi:10.1145/15886.15887. [S]
  Image-space booleans on a per-pixel depth list (the "dexel", a depth element) along the view
  direction. About 10 cuts a second on the hardware of the day.
- **Stifter, S. (1995). "Simulation of NC machining based on the dexel model: a critical
  analysis."** *IJAMT* 10. [S]
  The error analysis: a single direction of dexels misses walls parallel to it, which motivates
  tri-dexels.
- **Müller, Surmann, Stautner, Albersmann, Weinert (2003). "Online sculpting and visualization of
  multi-dexel volumes."** ACM SM '03, 258–261. [S] (dblp) Tri-dexel (three orthogonal dexel grids)
  and surface reconstruction.
- **Spence & Altintas (1994). "A solid modeller based milling process simulation and planning
  system."** *J. Eng. Ind.* 116(1), Feb 1994 (pages [K]). doi:10.1115/1.2901810. [S]
  Exact CSG: the cutter is intersected with each primitive along the path and the results combined
  through the CSG tree. The engagement and forces come from the result, and the feed is scheduled
  against torque, force and surface-error limits. El-Mounayri, Spence, Elbestawi (1998) extended it
  to 3-axis with a B-rep [S]. It is exact but the B-rep grows with every cut, the known scaling
  problem of exact stock models.
- **Sullivan, Erdim, Perry, Frisken (2012). "High accuracy NC milling simulation using composite
  adaptively sampled distance fields."** *CAD* 44(6):522–536. doi:10.1016/j.cad.2012.02.002. [R]
  https://www.merl.com/publications/TR2012-025
  The stock is a distance field, the result is the stock minus the swept volumes. Swept-volume
  distances are computed analytically in the tool frame, and an octree localises the work. It
  claims better than 1 µm accuracy, for 3- and 5-axis.
- **Z-map / height field** (Anderson 1978 [K]; Choi & Jerard 1998 [S]): one height per grid cell.
  It is exact at the samples for 3 axes but cannot hold undercuts.

**Accuracy versus time.** Exact B-rep (Spence) gives exact results but slows with every cut.
Z-map, dexel and voxel are fast but carry a sampling error. Their error at a sample is known; between
samples it is bounded by the tool's geometry (a sample spacing s with a ball of radius R misses at
most R − √(R² − s²/4)). ADFs reach µm accuracy with adaptive sampling, but the accuracy is
empirical, not certified.

**For an exact implementation.** forgeCAD already has the exact boolean, so 3-axis stock is
stock ∖ ⋃ (swept primitive) with no new mathematics, only the cost. A certified fast path is a
dexel grid that stores, per ray, an exact or interval-bounded set of intervals. Each dexel is a 1D
boolean of exact intervals. That gives exact answers on the rays and a stated bound between them,
which is the honest spec for a fast simulator.

## 8. Feeds and speeds

- **Taylor, F. W. (1907). "On the art of cutting metals."** *Trans. ASME* 28:31–350. [K]
  V·T^n = C: tool life T falls as a power of cutting speed V, with n and C fitted per tool and
  material pair.
- **Kienzle, O. (1952).** *VDI-Z* 94. [K] Specific cutting force k_c = k_c1.1·h^(−m_c): the force
  per area of chip rises as the chip gets thinner, with k_c1.1 the value at h = 1 mm, b = 1 mm.
- **Altintas, Y. (2000, 2nd ed. 2012). *Manufacturing Automation: Metal Cutting Mechanics, Machine
  Tool Vibrations, and CNC Design*.** Cambridge UP. [K]
  Mechanistic force model: the force on each tooth is cutting coefficients times the
  instantaneous chip area, plus edge forces, integrated over the engaged arc and the helix.
- **Altintas & Budak (1995). "Analytical prediction of stability lobes in milling."** *CIRP Annals*
  44(1):357–362. doi:10.1016/S0007-8506(07)62342-7. [S]
  The zeroth-order Fourier term of the time-varying directional coefficients gives the
  frequency-domain limit of axial depth against spindle speed (the stability lobes). Budak &
  Altintas 1998 added multi-frequency terms for low radial immersion [S].
- **Feed-rate optimisation from engagement: VERICUT OptiPath / Force** (CGTech).
  https://cgtech.com/products/product-line/100-optipath.html [S]
  It splits moves and sets each feed from the material removed (OptiPath) or from a constant chip
  thickness and force (Force). It rewrites feeds only and leaves the path as it is. The claimed
  20–70% gains are vendor figures. Spence & Altintas (1994) is the academic precursor.
- ***Machinery's Handbook*** (Oberg et al., Industrial Press, 31st ed. 2020). [K] The tables of
  speed and feed per material and tool, tool-life corrections, and the chip-thinning factor.

**For an exact implementation.** Every model here is empirical: fitted constants with a stated
scatter, often ±20% or more. Nothing here can be "proven" except the geometric inputs: engagement
angle, chip thickness from f_z and φ, and the material removal rate. Prove those exactly, and treat
the force, life and stability numbers as data with a units-checked formula, labelled as empirical
in the output. The stability lobe is a numerical eigenproblem; a certified version (intervals on
the eigenvalue) is possible but is research work and not worth it before the rest.

## 9. G-code and post-processing

- **ISO 6983-1:2009 / EIA RS-274-D.** [K] Word-address format: G0 rapid, G1 line, G2/G3 arc
  (centre by I J K, or R), G17–19 plane selection, G40–42 cutter compensation, G90/91 absolute or
  incremental. Dialects differ in many details.
- **Kramer, Proctor, Messina (2000). *The NIST RS274NGC Interpreter, Version 3*.** NISTIR 6556.
  doi:10.6028/NIST.IR.6556. [S] https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/Legacy/IR/nistir6556.pdf
  The fullest public semantics of a G-code dialect: RS274/NGC input in, calls to canonical
  machining functions out. It is the basis of EMC2/LinuxCNC. This is the natural object for a
  forgeCAD L1 spec of "what a program means".
- **STEP-NC: ISO 14649 (the ARM) and ISO 10303-238 (AP238, the AIM).** [S] Xu, Wang, Mao, Newman,
  Kramer, Proctor, Michaloski (2004), "STEP-compliant NC research: the search for intelligent
  CAD/CAPP/CAM/CNC integration," *IJPR* (NIST lists 2004; the volume, 43(17), is [K]). https://www.nist.gov/publications/step-compliant-nc-research-search-intelligent-cadcappcamcnc-integration
  The program describes features, operations and the workpiece geometry, not axis motions, so the
  controller has the exact target part. Real controller support is still rare.
- **Jerk-limited feed: Erkorkmaz & Altintas (2001). "High speed CNC system design. Part I: jerk
  limited trajectory generation and quintic spline interpolation."** *IJMTM* 41(9):1323–1345.
  doi:10.1016/S0890-6955(01)00002-5. [S]
  Quintic splines with continuous position, velocity and acceleration, a trapezoidal acceleration
  (jerk-limited) feed profile, and a correction for the feed fluctuation from the spline's
  parameter.
- **Arc fitting.** [K] Yeung & Walton (1994), "Curve fitting with arc splines for NC toolpath
  generation," *CAD* 26(11); Meek & Walton biarcs. Fitting G2/G3 to a polyline within a tolerance
  shortens programs and smooths motion.
- **NURBS output and interpolation.** [K] Shpitalni, Koren, Lo (1994), "Realtime curve
  interpolators," *CAD* 26(11). Fanuc G05.1/G06.2 and Siemens BSPLINE accept splines directly.

**For an exact implementation.** G0/G1/G2/G3 in a fixed plane are lines and arcs, the same algebra
as §1. A forgeCAD post that writes decimal words with a stated resolution (e.g. 0.001 mm) is a
rounding: the spec must say that the *rounded* program is gouge-free, not the exact path. That
needs either the check of §6 on the rounded program, or a proof that rounding moves the path by at
most δ and that the check used a margin of δ. Arc words have the problem that the centre and both
endpoints are given separately and can be inconsistent after rounding. Controllers accept a small
radius mismatch (LinuxCNC has a tolerance), and that tolerance must be in the spec.

## 10. Formal verification of CAM, toolpaths and NC code

The field is very thin. I found nothing that proves a toolpath generator correct or verifies a
gouge check in a proof assistant.

- **Sottile & Tekriwal (LLNL). "Design and implementation of a verified interpreter for additive
  manufacturing programs."** FUNARCH 2024 at ICFP. [R]
  https://icfp24.sigplan.org/details/funarch-2024-papers/3/Design-and-implementation-of-a-verified-interpreter-for-additive-manufacturing-progra
  A toolpath analyser for RS-274 (3D printing) with verified parts extracted to OCaml. The page does
  not name the prover.
- **Tekriwal & Sottile (2025). "Mechanized semantics for correctness of the RS274 additive
  manufacturing command language."** NASA Formal Methods (NFM) 2025. [S] (cited as ref. [4] in
  arXiv:2605.10437, read this session) and the dataset **"Basic RS274 Coq and Why3 semantics
  proofs,"** OSTI, Sept 2025 https://www.osti.gov/dataexplorer/biblio/dataset/2997258 [S].
  A mechanised semantics of RS274 in Coq and Why3. It is the closest precedent for a forgeCAD L1
  spec of G-code.
- **He, Nandi, Pai (2025). "Formalizing linear motion G-code for invariant checking and
  differential testing of fabrication tools."** arXiv:2509.00699 (31 Aug 2025; a search summary says
  OOPSLA 2025 [S]). [R] https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.00699
  It lifts linear G-code to a set of cuboids, approximated by a point cloud. The tool is
  GlitchFinder, which compares slicers and checks for faults introduced by mesh repair. This is
  semantics for testing, not mechanised proof.
- **Lee, Y. (2026). "Separation logic for verifying physical collisions of CNC programs."**
  arXiv:2605.10437 (May 2026). [R] https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.10437
  The workspace is a "spatial heap", and a collision is a failure of the separating conjunction (a
  "spatial data race"). Trajectories are discretised before the logic runs, and the proof rules are
  on paper, with no proof assistant. The idea is suggestive, but the discretisation is exactly the
  unproven step.
- **Robust offset and Voronoi in practice** (not proofs): Held VRONI and ArcVRONI (§1), CGAL straight
  skeleton and exact Minkowski offsets (§1), and the CGAL segment Delaunay graph (Karavelas 2004
  [K]). These are exact-predicate or careful-floating-point *implementations*, not verified ones.
  The "classroom examples of robustness problems" (Kettner, Mehlhorn, Pion, Schirra, Yap 2008,
  *CGTA* 40 [K]) show why floating-point offsets fail.
- **Proof-assistant geometry that would carry over** [K]: verified convex hulls and Delaunay
  (Isabelle, Coq: Pichardie & Bertot 2001; Dufourd and Bertot's combinatorial maps), CoRN and
  mathlib real analysis, and interval arithmetic in Coq (Melquiond's `Interval`) and Isabelle
  (Hölzl's approximation). Lean 4 mathlib has convexity, measure and Minkowski-sum pieces but no
  machining work that I know of.

## Implications for forgeCAD

**1. What can be exact (L2 over ℚ or quadratic extensions).**
- *2.5D offsets of lines and arcs.* The trimmed offset at distance d is again lines and arcs. Its
  vertices are line–line, line–circle or circle–circle intersections, so they lie in quadratic
  extensions of the input field. The trim is a regularised union with a disc, the boolean forgeCAD
  already proves. Degree grows with repeated offsets: either bound the number of passes, keep a
  representation of the tower of square roots (Alg/Quad), or offset from the *original* boundary
  each time (offset_{kd}(P), not offset_d^k(P)), which keeps every pass at degree 2 over the input.
  The second form is also the correct definition. Mitred (straight-skeleton) offsets are rational,
  but they are not a round tool's centre path. Use them only where mitred is meant.
- *Rest and uncut regions in 2D.* The opening by a disc is an erosion and a dilation, so exact.
- *Engagement angle* against a line-and-arc stock. Intersections of circles are exact; keep cos φ
  or the chord, not the angle in radians.
- *3-axis swept volumes* of flat, ball, bull and APT tools along G1 and planar G2/G3: their bounding
  surfaces are planes, cylinders, cones, spheres and tori, all inside the existing curved-boolean
  scope (quadrics are at L1 today; tori are needed for bull tools and arc moves).
- *Drop-cutter* of cylinder, ball and cone tools against points, segments, planes and spheres:
  square roots and quadratics. Bull against an edge is a quartic, which is exact as an algebraic
  number but needs root isolation.

**2. What needs certified intervals (L5 predicates plus a Krawczyk or interval-Newton step).**
- Drop-cutter and waterline against freeform (spline) faces, and bull-nose contacts.
- Helical moves (G2/G3 with Z), 5-axis moves with rotary axes, and the controller's joint-space
  interpolation, whose sweeps are transcendental.
- Continuous collision of holder and spindle against the stock and fixtures over a whole move
  (conservative advancement on a bound of the motion's speed).
The rule from the proof standard applies: one decided subdivision rule and one depth budget, with
the result "undecided" reported as a refusal and not retried with a looser setting.

**3. The honest spec for "gouge-free".**
Let P be the target part (closed, regular), with an optional allowance a ≥ 0 (roughing leaves a).
Let T be the tool's cutting solid and H the holder and spindle. Let the program be the motions
g_i : [0,1] → SE(3) *as the stated controller interpolates them*: lines and arcs for 3 axes, and
joint-linear motion through the stated machine kinematics for 5 axes. RTCP is a different
interpolation, so the spec names which one. Let S = ⋃_i ⋃_t g_i(t)·T be the swept tool.
- **Gouge-free:** S ∩ int(P ⊕ B_a) = ∅.
- **Holder-safe:** for every i and t, g_i(t)·H ∩ (Stock_i(t) ∪ Fixtures) = ∅, where Stock_i(t) is
  the stock as it stands at that moment. The order of the moves matters here, so this is not just a
  check against the final part.
- **Rapids:** every G0 sweep with the *whole* tool assembly misses the current stock and the
  fixtures.
In Lean this is a statement about subsets of ℝ³ and the regularised boolean already in
`reg_kept`. An L3 check can be sound and *complete within its domain* for 3-axis lines and arcs
(exact surfaces). For 5-axis and freeform it can only be sound, with an explicit "undecided"
answer. The spec must say that, rather than claim a decision procedure.

**4. The honest spec for "stock − swept = target within tolerance".**
Let R = cl(int(Stock ∖ S)) be the regularised remaining material. The requirement is two one-sided
inclusions:
- P ⊆ R (equivalent to gouge-free for a = 0), and
- R ⊆ P ⊕ B_ε (no material more than ε outside the part: the undercut or scallop bound).
That is a one-sided Hausdorff condition. A certified fast simulator uses two different
approximations of S: an *outer* enclosure S⁺ ⊇ S for the first inclusion (if S⁺ misses int P,
then so does S), and an *inner* one S⁻ ⊆ S for the second (if Stock ∖ S⁻ ⊆ P ⊕ B_ε, then so is
R). Dexels or ADFs give these only if their sampling error is bounded and is added in the
conservative direction. A plain Z-map gives neither. The scallop height (§4) is then a *derived*
lemma that bounds ε for a given step-over and curvature, not part of the spec.

**5. Where approximation is unavoidable, and how to state it.**
- *Non-developable flank milling*: an exact cut is impossible (§5), so the spec is an error bound.
- *Empirical cutting models* (Taylor, Kienzle, mechanistic coefficients, stability lobes): these
  are data, not theorems. Prove the geometry they take in, units-check the formula, and mark the
  outputs as empirical.
- *Output rounding*: the program written in decimal is not the path computed. Either check the
  rounded program, or carry a proven rounding margin δ into the gouge check (use P ⊕ B_δ).
- *The machine*: servo lag, deflection, thermal growth and the controller's look-ahead are outside
  any geometric spec. State the spec as "relative to the stated interpolation model".
- *Path generation is optimisation* (spirals, adaptive clearing, orientation choice). Do not prove
  it optimal. Prove its output satisfies §3 and §4, which is how the boolean is already handled:
  the algorithm can be anything, and the checked result is what is proven.

**Suggested order.** (a) An L1 spec of the motion semantics for G0/G1/G2/G3 in a plane, following
RS274/NGC (Kramer 2000; Tekriwal & Sottile's Coq work is the precedent). (b) An L1 spec of S, of
gouge-free, and of the two-inclusion tolerance. (c) An L2 exact 2.5D pocket: an exact line-and-arc
offset plus the boolean, and a checker for the spec in (b) using 3-axis swept primitives. (d)
Certified enclosures for helical, freeform and 5-axis moves, behind the same checker. Toolpath
*generators* (offset, Held/Abrahamsen spirals, adaptive clearing) can then be anything, as long as
their output passes (c) or (d).
